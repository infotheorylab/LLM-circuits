An LLM classifier is a noisy measurement instrument

Prompt a language model with a sample and it returns a label. Ask again and it may return a different one. Each query is a noisy measurement of the true label, and each measurement has a price.

Here a classifier is one configuration: a backend model, an instruction prompt, a rubric, decoding settings. We call each configuration an instrument. A pool of instruments differs in accuracy, in cost per call, and in which prompts each one gets right.

The question. Given a pool of instruments, which should we query next, if any, and when should we stop and predict?

Question 1

Which circuit is optimal?

Among all ways of wiring a pool of classifiers together, which one gives the best accuracy at a given average cost?

Question 2

Can we learn it from data?

The optimal circuit needs probabilities we do not know. Can a small model estimate them for unseen prompts?

Routing, cascades and voting are all circuits

A classification circuit is any procedure that queries one or more instruments and combines their outputs into a final decision. The instruments are the components; decision rules say which to query next, when to stop, and how to combine.

Majority voting, routing and cascades are three wirings of the same components, each with its own cost and accuracy. LMCircuit searches the whole class, letting every choice depend on the prompt and on the answers so far.

errorE(π) = probability the final prediction is wrong
costC(π) = expected price of the calls the circuit makes
objectiveminimize E(π) + λ·C(π)
λ ≥ 0the price of cost relative to error: λ = 0 only maximizes accuracy, while a large λ does not query any instrument

At every step, query or predict?

Any circuit that queries several instruments at once has a sequential twin with the same error and the same cost. So the optimal circuit can always ask one instrument at a time, and the whole design reduces to a sequence of query-or-stop decisions.

The circuit keeps a belief about the label. Stopping costs the Bayes error of that belief, min(q, 1−q). Querying instrument i costs λci now, but reduces the Bayes error.

The Bellman equation compares the two. Solving it backward using a dynamic program over all states gives the optimal circuit for this prompt and this lambda.

We learn one probabilistic model over instruments and prompts.

The dynamic program needs two things for every prompt: the belief P(Y | z, history) at every state, and the chance that each instrument says safe. We learn both from a labeled training set.

A text embedding φ(z) enters a small network fθ. The label head η reads the embedding together with the answers collected so far and returns the belief. The instrument heads νi predict what each instrument will say.

The heads feed the dynamic program: η sets the stopping error at each state, ν sets the transition probabilities. The network runs on a CPU, and at the empty state it already gives a zero-cost prediction.

A better cost–accuracy frontier on seven tasks

Safety (BeaverTails), health (HealthBench), political science (PolitiCAUSE), human preference (LMArena), agent evaluation (AgentJudgeBench), decision-making with System One models (Decision Index) and reasoning (BIG-Bench Hard). The instruments range from sub-billion-parameter open models to frontier APIs. Across all seven, the learned circuits attain higher accuracy than every individual instrument and than the routing and cascading baselines at nearly all budgets: RouteLLM, HybridLLM, CARROT, Cascade Routing, Consistency Cascade, Cascaded Selective, FrugalGPT and Adaptive-Consistency.

Circuits of LLM Classifiers

Abstract. Language models make it easy to build classifiers by simply prompting a model with the sample to classify. However, their predictions are neither deterministic nor uniformly reliable across samples. How can we combine predictions from different models to improve reliability? We view LLM classifiers as noisy measurement instruments of an underlying target label. Our goal is to maximize average classification accuracy at a given average query cost. To this end, we introduce circuits that organize LLM classifiers into an adaptive sequence of queries and decisions. We then characterize the optimal circuit, obtain the fundamental limits of achievable accuracy, and provide practical insights on realizing the optimal circuit. Across seven classification tasks spanning safety, health, political science, human preference, agent evaluation, decision-making and reasoning, our method improves the cost–accuracy frontier over routing and cascading baselines.

LMCircuit, technically

For a prompt z, a state s is the set of (instrument, answer) pairs observed so far. The belief is qz(s) = P(Y = 1 | z, s) and the stopping error is ez(s) = min{qz(s), 1 − qz(s)}. With a price λ on cost, the value of a state satisfies

Vλ,z(s) = min { ez(s) ,  mini ∉ M(s) [ λci(z) + Σx ∈ {0,1} Q[i](x | z) · Vλ,z(s ∪ {(i, x)}) ] }
stop and predict   vs.   pay for instrument i, then continue from the answer
01

Sequential without loss. Any circuit that queries a batch of instruments at once has a sequential counterpart with the same error and cost, so the search can be restricted to one query at a time.

02

Conditional independence. Each call uses fresh randomness, so measurements are modeled as independent given the prompt. Error correlation across prompts is a separate matter, and the embedding carries it: without test labels, the correlations between instruments' answers, and between their errors, implied by the fitted model are within 4–17% of the measured values, and its implied accuracy of each instrument is within 1.2–3.6%.

03

Belief model. fθ(φ(z), s) = (ηθ(φ(z), s), νθ(1)(φ(z), s), …, νθ(n)(φ(z), s)). A learned projection of a 1024-dimensional Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B vector to 64 dimensions feeds three small heads. The model assumes each prompt belongs to one of T latent types: a type head gives the prior over types, a measurement head gives each instrument's chance of answering 1 under each type, and a label head gives the label probability under each type at state s, summing learned embeddings of the observed (instrument, answer) pairs so it is invariant to their order. The observed answers update the type by Bayes' rule and the outputs average over types. T ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8} is chosen on validation.

04

Loss. For each training prompt we draw a random state (a random subset of instruments and one recorded answer each) and minimize binary cross-entropy of η against the recorded labels, weighted by annotator counts where several annotators labeled a prompt (as on HealthBench), plus cross-entropy of each unqueried νi against every recorded call of instrument i. AdamW with early stopping on validation; one temperature per head is then fit on validation states; five seeds per fold.

05

Costs. ci(z) is known before the query. On the four tasks we query ourselves it is the input tokens of z times the input price, plus the 95th percentile of the instrument's output length on training prompts times the output price. On AgentJudgeBench, BIG-Bench Hard and Decision Index it is the instrument's mean recorded cost on training prompts. Costs are divided by the mean cost of the most expensive instrument.

06

Frontier. The dynamic program is solved for 201 prices spaced geometrically from 10−8 to 102, plus λ = 0 and λ = ∞. Each dataset is split into five folds, keeping prompts that share a source together. For each budget the price with the best validation balanced accuracy within budget is selected and its test balanced accuracy reported.

07

Pool selection. The gain from adding an instrument is at most √(I(Y; Xnew | Xpool, Z)/2). Starting from a cheap instrument and greedily adding the one with the largest conditional information reaches the full-pool frontier with 2–3 instruments on the four tasks shown in the paper, and dominates accuracy-first, cheapest-first and random orderings.

08

Scaling laws. For a fixed prompt, the optimal error decays exponentially with expected cost: −log Eλ(z) / Cλ(z) → I(z), where I(z) is the largest information rate in the pool (KL divergence between an instrument's answer distributions under the two labels, per unit cost), and the circuit spends almost all of its cost on the instruments that attain it. Across a population of prompts the rate becomes 1 / E[1/I(Z)]; if too many prompts have rates near zero, the decay slows to stretched-exponential or polynomial.

Baselines

Predictive routers pick one model per prompt. Cascades query models in a fixed order until a confidence rule fires. Sampling repeats one model until its answers agree. All are circuits, so the dynamic program searches over them.
